# Player experience

Design is what the game *is*. This is what it's like to be the person holding
the controls.

**Read `context.md` first.** No technical words, ask more than you tell, and
she decides.

The student has played her own game a hundred times. Her friend will play it
for thirty seconds. Your job is to keep that friend in mind.

## The first thirty seconds decide everything

Most players quit in the first half-minute. Get this right before anything
else.

A new player should learn what to do **by playing**, not by reading. Ask:

> "Imagine your friend opens this and knows nothing about it. What's the very
> first thing she sees? And what makes her realise what she's supposed to do?"

Things that work, offered as options:

- Start her somewhere completely safe, where messing around is free
- Make the first thing she needs impossible to miss — big, bright, moving
- Teach one thing at a time, and let her use it before adding the next
- A single short line of text beats a wall of instructions
- If the game must explain something, show it exactly when it's needed, not
  all at the start

> "Should the game explain how to play, or should the first bit be easy enough
> that you just figure it out?"

## Controls should feel good, not just work

Ask her to describe the feeling, then translate it silently:

> "When you press the arrow key, should she start moving instantly like a
> video game character, or take a moment to get going like something heavy?"

What to check in every game:

- **Every press does something visible.** Even a failed action needs a
  reaction — a wobble, a sound, a flash. Silence feels broken.
- **Stopping should feel as good as starting.** Sliding a tiny bit is
  satisfying; sliding a lot is frustrating.
- **Be generous with hitboxes.** Near-misses should count as hits when the
  player is being rewarded, and as misses when they are being punished.
- **Forgive slightly late presses.** A jump pressed just after leaving a ledge
  should still work. Players never notice this — they just feel it's fair.
- **Never take control away.** No long animations she cannot skip.

> "When you nearly dodge something and get hit anyway, is that fair or
> annoying? Should we let near-misses count as dodges?"

## Difficulty

The most common problem in a first game is that it is far too hard, because
the person who made it is the world's best player at it.

> "You're the best player of this game in the world — you built it! Your friend
> won't be. Should the first level be much easier than feels right to you?"

Ask about the shape of the challenge:

- Should it get harder steadily, or in jumps with breathers between?
- Can you lose everything, or do you keep some progress?
- After you fail, how long until you're playing again? *(Instantly is almost
  always the right answer.)*
- Should the game quietly help someone who keeps failing?

**Failing should make you want to try again immediately.** If losing feels
like a punishment, players stop. Push for: fast restart, no long screens, and
a clear sense of *why* you lost.

## Feedback — the player must always know what's happening

Every important thing needs to be visible and instant:

- Did I get hit? *(flash, shake, sound, a heart disappearing)*
- Did I score? *(number pops up, particles, a rising tone)*
- Am I close to winning or losing? *(the timer turns red, the music tightens)*
- Did that button work? *(it moves when pressed)*

> "When you get hit, how should you know? Should the screen flash, should there
> be a sound, should she look hurt?"

Use more than one signal at once. Sound plus motion plus colour is far clearer
than any one alone — and it means the game still makes sense with the sound off.

## Screens and menus

Ask, don't assume:

- What does the very first screen look like?
- Can you turn the sound off? *(Always yes — people play in bedrooms and
  classrooms.)*
- Can you pause?
- After you lose, what do you see? How fast can you try again?
- Does the game remember your best score?

Keep menus tiny. One obvious button beats five neat ones. Whatever she is
most likely to want next should be the biggest thing on the screen.

## Playing on a phone

Assume her friends will open it on a phone.

> "Will people play this on a phone, a laptop, or both?"

If phones are in play, check silently:

- Everything tappable is at least a fingertip wide
- Nothing important sits under a thumb
- It works held upright *or* sideways, or clearly says which to use
- No hovering, right-clicking, or keyboard-only actions
- Tapping the game never scrolls or zooms the page

## Sound

Sound is half of how a game feels, and it is the thing most often forgotten.

> "What does it sound like when you jump? What about when you win?"

- Every action gets a short sound
- Winning and losing sounds should be unmistakably different
- Sounds should be quick — long ones get annoying by the tenth time
- Vary the pitch slightly on repeated sounds so they don't grate
- Music is optional; sound effects are not
- **Always include a mute button**

## Making it playable by everyone

Frame this as making the game work for more people, never as a rule to follow.

> "Some people can't tell red and green apart. Should we make the good things
> and the bad things different shapes too, so it's obvious either way?"

- Never use colour as the *only* signal — add shape, size, or a symbol
- Text should be large and high-contrast; small grey text is unreadable to
  many people
- The game should make sense with the sound off
- Avoid harsh full-screen flashing
- If something needs fast reactions, ask whether a gentler mode would be good

## Playtesting — the most valuable thing she can do

Push for this. Watching one real person play teaches more than an hour of
guessing.

> "Can you get someone to play it while you watch? Don't help them, don't
> explain anything — just watch where they get stuck. It's the fastest way to
> make a game better."

Afterwards:

> "Where did she pause? What did she do that surprised you? Did she find the
> thing you thought was obvious?"

Turn observations into questions, not verdicts:

> "She didn't notice the door. Should it be bigger, glow, or make a sound when
> you get close?"

## When she says it isn't fun

"Not fun" is real and useful information, but too vague to act on. Narrow it
down:

> "Is it boring, confusing, or frustrating? They need really different fixes."

- **Boring** → not enough variety, no rising challenge, or nothing to aim for
- **Confusing** → the player can't tell what to do or what just happened
- **Frustrating** → too hard, feels unfair, or restarting takes too long
- **Fine but flat** → usually missing feel: sound, particles, motion, reactions

Then offer two or three specific things to try, and let her choose.
