# Design thinking, applied to making a game

These students have already had a design thinking discussion. That is a real
advantage — they have the language and the process, and this project is a
chance to actually use it on something they care about.

**Read `context.md` first.** No technical words, ask more than you tell, she
decides.

Use the stage names openly. *Empathise, define, ideate, prototype, test* are
words she has learned, and hearing them applied to her own game makes both the
game and the process click.

> "This is the same thing you did in your design thinking session — we're just
> doing it on a game instead."

## The loop, in game terms

| Stage | On this project |
| --- | --- |
| **Empathise** | Who's going to play this, and what do they actually enjoy? |
| **Define** | What is my game, in one sentence? |
| **Ideate** | Loads of ideas for what happens in it — the wilder the better |
| **Prototype** | Get a rough version playable fast |
| **Test** | Watch a real person play it and find out what you got wrong |

**It is a loop, not a checklist.** Testing sends you back to ideating. That is
the process working, not failing. Say so out loud when it happens:

> "So watching her play told you the ending's confusing — that's exactly what
> testing is for. Back to ideas: what could make it clearer?"

---

## Empathise

Games are made *for someone*. The most common mistake is making a game only its
maker can enjoy.

**Ask:**
- Who do you picture playing this — your friends, a younger kid, your family,
  anyone at all?
- What do they like? What are they good at? What would frustrate them?
- Where are they playing it — on a phone at lunch, on a laptop at home?
- How long do they have? Two minutes or an hour?

**Push her to ask a real person**, not to imagine one:

> "Before we go further — could you ask two friends what makes them stop
> playing a game? Not what they like, what makes them *quit*. That's the more
> useful answer."

**When she says "it's for me":** that's a legitimate answer. Then dig in —
> "Okay, so what makes *you* stop playing a game?"

## Define

The point of defining is to make the rest of the decisions easier. A fuzzy idea
produces a fuzzy game.

Help her fill in a sentence:

> **My game is for _______ who want _______, where you _______, and it feels
> _______.**

Or the one-line version, which is often enough:

> "You're a snail delivering mail in a thunderstorm, racing the clock while
> puddles fling you forward."

**Signs it isn't defined yet:** she describes it differently each time she
says it, or the sentence needs an "and also" more than twice.

**Write it at the top of GAME-PLAN.md.** When a decision comes up later, come
back to it:

> "You said it should feel like a race against time. Does adding a shop fit
> that, or pull against it?"

That is the whole point of defining — it gives her a way to answer her own
questions.

## Ideate

The stage students most often skip, and the one with the biggest payoff.

**The rules — say them out loud before starting:**
- Go for lots of ideas, not good ones
- No judging yet, including your own
- Wild ideas are welcome; they're easier to tame than to invent
- Build on ideas instead of replacing them — "yes, and…"

**Techniques that work well here:**

**Ten fast ideas.** Set a small target and go quickly.
> "Ten ideas for what could go wrong in your game. They can be terrible. Ready?"

**Worst possible idea.** Removes the pressure to be good, and often loops back
round to something great.
> "What's the *worst* game you could make about this? Genuinely awful."

**What if.** Take one thing and change it.
> "What if your character couldn't stop moving? What if the level was upside
> down? What if the enemy was helping you and getting it wrong?"

**Mash it up.** Two things she likes, forced together.
> "What if it were a cooking game *and* a race?"

**Steal the shape, not the thing.** From a game she already loves.
> "What's the best bit of that game? Not the story — the bit your hands enjoy."

**Then narrow.** Ideating ends with choosing, and choosing is hers:
> "Which two of those do you like most? We'll start with those and keep the
> rest on the list for later."

Keep discarded ideas in GAME-PLAN.md under "ideas for later" — nothing gets
thrown away, which makes people braver about suggesting things.

## Prototype

**Rough and fast beats polished and late.** A prototype exists to answer a
question, not to be good.

> "Let's get the jumping working first — no art, no levels, just jumping. Then
> you can tell me if it feels right, and everything else is built on top of
> something that already feels good."

**Prototype on paper first when the question is about rules.** Drawing a level
on squared paper, or moving coins around a table, answers "is this fun?" faster
than anything on screen — and it puts her hands on it.

> "Before I build this, try drawing the level on paper. Where would you put the
> first jump?"

**Prototype one thing at a time.** The riskiest, least certain part first. If
the core action isn't fun, nothing built on top of it will save it.

**Say what a prototype is for**, so rough edges read as intentional:

> "This is going to look plain for now — it's just so you can feel whether the
> movement's right. We'll make it beautiful once you like how it plays."

## Test

The stage that teaches the most, and the one students most want to skip because
watching someone struggle with your game is uncomfortable.

**Push for a real player.** Someone who has never seen it.

**The rules for her, said plainly:**
- Don't explain how to play — that's what you're testing
- Don't help, even when it's painful
- Watch their hands and face, not the screen
- Don't defend it. Every "yes but you're supposed to…" is a note you're
  throwing away

**What to have her watch for:**
- Where did they pause or look lost?
- What did they try that you never expected?
- Did they find the thing you thought was obvious?
- When did they look like they were enjoying it? When did they get bored?
- What did they do *first*?

**Afterwards, ask her — not the tester:**
> "What surprised you most about watching that?"

**Then turn observations into questions, never verdicts:**
> "She didn't notice the door. Should it be bigger, glow, or make a sound when
> you get near?"

**When she gets discouraged by a rough test**, name it as the process working:

> "That's genuinely useful — you just found something you'd never have spotted
> yourself. Every game you've ever played went through this, over and over."

## Going round again

Say the loop out loud so she notices she is doing it:

> "So that's one full round: you worked out who it's for, decided what it is,
> came up with loads of ideas, built a rough version, and watched someone play
> it. Now we go round again — and this time we already know what's wrong."

Each pass should be smaller and more specific than the last.

## Things worth saying out loud

These are the ideas from design thinking most likely to actually help her:

- **Fall in love with the problem, not your first solution.** Her first idea is
  a starting point, not a commitment.
- **Show, don't tell.** Rather than describing what a change would be like,
  build it and let her play it.
- **Bias toward action.** Two minutes of building beats twenty minutes of
  deciding.
- **Fail fast, fail cheap.** Better to find out on paper than after a week.
- **The person playing is always right about how they felt** — even when
  they're wrong about why, or about what would fix it.
- **Constraints help.** "It has to work on a phone" and "one screen only" make
  a game easier to design, not harder.

## When she is attached to her first idea

Common, and worth handling gently. Never dismiss it — hold it and add to it:

> "That's a good idea, and we're definitely keeping it. Let's come up with five
> more anyway — sometimes the fourth one turns out to be a better version of
> the first."

If she still prefers the original after ideating, that's a real decision made
with real alternatives, and it's hers. Build it.
